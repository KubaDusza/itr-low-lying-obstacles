# I. Introduction

Safe navigation in indoor environments requires mobile robots to detect not only large obstacles such as walls and furniture, but also very small and low-lying objects such as cables, latex gloves, screws, and other floor-level hazards. These obstacles are particularly challenging because they occupy few pixels in the image and may also be difficult to distinguish from the floor using depth sensors. Previous work has therefore explored both appearance-based methods using RGB images and geometric methods using RGB-D or stereo depth information. Hua et al. showed that combining RGB and depth can improve small-obstacle segmentation for robotic navigation, although the obstacles considered in their experiments were generally larger than thin or small objects such as cables, latex gloves, or screws \cite{hua2019small}.

The current objective of this project is to evaluate whether combining RGB information with stereo depth improves the detection of thin and low-lying indoor obstacles compared with using RGB or depth alone.  
As a secondary objective, we evaluate whether pseudo-labels generated by a foundation model (SAM) can replace manual annotation for training the lightweight learned models.

# II. State of the Art

Hua et al. \cite{hua2019small} proposed a two-stage RGB-D semantic segmentation system for small-obstacle avoidance. The first stage identifies the traversable road or floor region, while the second stage detects obstacles inside this region. Separate RGB and depth branches are used to extract and combine visual and geometric information. Their work demonstrates the usefulness of RGB-D sensing for obstacle perception, but their custom dataset mainly contains obstacles substantially larger than very thin floor hazards such as cables.

Chen et al. \cite{chen2023self} proposed MOSTS, a lightweight one-shot texture segmentation model for indoor robot navigation. Their system first estimates the ground plane from depth, point-cloud, and IMU information using filtering, RANSAC, and PCA, and then applies RGB texture segmentation to identify traversable floor regions. The authors note that their geometric ground-plane method typically detects objects higher than approximately 5 cm, meaning that very low objects may be missed by depth sensing. MOSTS addresses this limitation by comparing a query image with a reference image of the desired floor texture. On the ISOD dataset, MOSTS with a MobileNetV3 backbone achieves 85.7% mIoU while remaining suitable for real-time operation. A limitation is its dependence on a reference floor texture, which may reduce robustness in environments containing several valid floor appearances.

Mantegazza et al. \cite{mantegazza2023hazards} released Hazards&Robots, a dataset of RGB frames recorded by a ground robot in corridors with deliberately placed floor hazards, including cables, tape, and screws. However, it provides only frame-level labels and no depth, so segmenting the obstacles requires post-processing the flagged frames with SAM.

Caporali et al. \cite{caporali2023rt} presented RT-DLO, a real-time instance-segmentation method designed specifically for deformable linear objects such as cables, wires, and ropes. The method first obtains a binary segmentation mask and then exploits the linear structure of cables using a graph-based representation to separate individual instances, including at intersections. However, RT-DLO is designed mainly for robotic manipulation and operates on RGB images without using depth information.

Kirillov et al. \cite{kirillov2023segment} introduced the Segment Anything Model (SAM), a promptable foundation model for image segmentation with strong zero-shot transfer capabilities. SAM can generate object masks from point, box, mask, or other prompts and can therefore be useful as a general segmentation baseline or as an annotation tool. However, the authors identify fine structures and small disconnected components as limitations, which is especially relevant for small and thin floor obstacles.

Faria et al. \cite{faria2026sam3} demonstrated another useful role for foundation models: instead of deploying a computationally expensive model directly, SAM 3 can be used offline to generate pseudo-labels for training lightweight YOLO models. Their approach achieved a large reduction in inference latency while avoiding manual annotation during training. Although their application is pig detection rather than robotic navigation, the same idea could be useful for automatically assisting the annotation of obstacle datasets before training a lightweight segmentation model.

Together, these studies suggest that neither RGB nor depth alone is guaranteed to solve the problem of very small indoor obstacles. Depth-based methods provide useful geometric information but may fail when objects are extremely low, while RGB-based methods can detect appearance differences but may struggle with low contrast, fine structures, or complex floor textures. Therefore, the proposed project will compare RGB-only, depth-only, and RGB-D approaches under the same conditions, with particular attention to thin, flat, and small obstacles such as cables, gloves, and screws. This comparison will help determine whether stereo depth provides a measurable benefit over lightweight RGB segmentation and under which environmental conditions that benefit is most significant, and whether foundation-model pseudo-labels are sufficient for training the learned models.
